# Play In Editor, captures, exposure, and recording footage

Verified on Unreal Engine 5.9.0 (changelist 60351404, the launcher's install) on macOS 26.6, Apple Silicon (M4 Max), from 10 to 17 November 2026, while building two small games: a sports game, then a runner made for a game jam. Where a line comes from reading the engine source rather than from a run, it says so. Names starting `game.` or `GAME_` stand in for that game's console variables and actor labels: choose your own.

## 1. Play and capture

- `EditorToolset.EditorAppToolset.StartPIE {}`: in the viewport, 2 s warm-up, 30 s limit. Options (`bSimulate`, `playMode`, `startTransform`, `warmupSeconds`) go in an `options` object. `StopPIE {}` errors if nothing is running.
- `SceneCaptureToolset.SceneCaptureToolset.CaptureGameViewport {"world": null, "maxResolution": 1600}` captures PIE with the HUD drawn. `ListWorlds {}` shows the editor and PIE worlds (`/Game/Maps/UEDPIE_0_<Map>`).
- Capture rate over MCP: 130 frames at 960 px in 41.7 s, about 3 a second. With time dilation 0.25 that is 12.5 frames per second of game time: enough for a watchable clip, encoded with `ffmpeg -framerate 12.5`.

- **Capture shape changes between launches.** After a relaunch the viewport was wider than 16:9 where before it was squarer, so the black bars moved from top and bottom to the sides. Crop by comparing the two ratios (`scripts/crop169.py` does); a crop that assumes one case silently keeps the bars.
- **Sharp captures:** set the post-process volume's motion blur amount to 0, or a paused frame of a fast clip is smeared.
- **`SceneCaptureToolset.Capture` is not a look review.** The offscreen capture from an arbitrary camera (`world={"refPath": "/Game/Maps/UEDPIE_0_<Map>.<Map>"}`, `camera={location, rotation, fOV}`, `mode="Lit"`, `options={width, height}`) has no temporal history: the same scene came back blown out, with black shadows. Use it for geometry and framing only.

## 2. Catching a moment

- **Freeze a moment to capture it.** A call-out that lasts under a second cannot be caught by `CaptureGameViewport` on demand. From a script, poll for it (`await asyncio.sleep(0)` in a loop), then `unreal.GameplayStatics.set_game_paused(world, True)`: world time stops, the HUD keeps drawing, and the capture can be taken by the next call. `slomo 0.3` widens the window; `game.Test.Launch` stages the ball that causes the call.

## 3. Exposure and framing

- Exposure. With default auto exposure and a 600 lux sun the first capture was washed out. An unbound `PostProcessVolume` with manual exposure fixed it, converged by measuring the mean luminance of the cropped capture:

| `auto_exposure_bias` (manual) | Mean luma |
|---|---|
| -2.0 | 0.03 |
| 1.5 | 0.16 |
| 2.5 | 0.26 |
| 3.5 | 0.41 |

```python
grade = actors.spawn_actor_from_class(unreal.PostProcessVolume, unreal.Vector(0, 0, 0))
grade.set_editor_property("unbound", True)
settings = grade.get_editor_property("settings")
settings.set_editor_property("override_auto_exposure_method", True)
settings.set_editor_property("auto_exposure_method", unreal.AutoExposureMethod.AEM_MANUAL)
settings.set_editor_property("override_auto_exposure_bias", True)
settings.set_editor_property("auto_exposure_bias", 3.5)
grade.set_editor_property("settings", settings)   # write the struct back
```

- Fixed exposure for the sunset level, by measuring captures: bias 3.25 gave mean luma 0.39, 3.45 gave 0.42, 3.6 gave 0.54.
- Matching a camera to a reference image: derive a first guess from the reference (where the near and far court edges sit in the frame gives distance, height, pitch and field of view), capture, then blend the reference over the capture at 50 percent. The gym's first guess (height 418 cm, pitch 13.2 degrees, horizontal field of view 34 degrees, 1080 cm behind the baseline) needed no second pass.
- **Field of view.** A camera's field of view is horizontal for a 16:9 frame, and the engine keeps that frame's vertical angle. In a squarer viewport the view is narrower than the number suggests: frame subjects by the vertical half-angle, `atan(tan(fov/2) * 9/16)`.
- **Judge framing and whole-frame brightness from a real 16:9 frame, not from a crop of the level viewport.** Run on 5.9.0, 12 November 2026, with a game camera not constrained to an aspect ratio (`AspectRatio_MajorAxisFOV`). The first packaged build showed that the cropped editor captures everyone had judged were the middle 64% of the real frame: everything had been framed 1.57 times too close, and the hero was 0.26 of the frame's height where the captures had made him look about 0.4. The real frame also shows more dark surround, so its whole-frame mean luma was 0.03 to 0.12 lower than the crops had measured. Why the crop came out that narrow was not traced. A camera constrained to 16:9 can still be cropped (`scripts/crop169.py`); for anything else take the frame from a floating 1920 x 1080 play window (section 5) or an off-screen `-game` run.
- **Compare a look with its mockup by the spread of brightness, not by mean luma alone.** The owner called a level "quite dark and dull" when its mean was close to the mockup's: 19% of its pixels were near black against the mockup's 6%. After the fix, 7%. Measure the near-black and near-white shares, the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles, and the hero's saturation against the world's.
- **Floors and horizons.** A basic-shape cylinder scaled to 3 km rendered unlit black; at 40 m it lit normally. Past the edge of any floor the view below the horizon is black unless the level has height fog.

## 4. Frame rate

- **Cost of the real scene.** The greybox gym ran at the editor's 120 frames a second; the dressed level with 20 Nanite meshes, two skinned characters and hardware ray tracing ran at 64 to 68; with volumetric cloud and haze, about 42.
- **Frame rate is only comparable on a quiet machine.** The same scene read 12 frames per second while four Blender renders and a video encode ran, 37 a minute later, and 42 to 46 once they had finished. Count frames yourself (`SystemLibrary.get_frame_count()` over game time) and check `ps` for other renderers before blaming your change.

- **Renamed editors hide from the quiet-machine check.** An editor or `-game` run started under its own `argv[0]` (as this skill advises) does not match `pgrep UnrealEditor`; one such recording editor loaded the graphics chip through six frame-rate runs, and one run spread from 20 to 74 ms a frame. List every process whose name ends in `Editor` or `Game` before measuring.
- **Measure a packaged or `-game` run's frame cost with the CSV profiler.** `-ExecCmds="csvprofile frames=400"` writes `Saved/Profiling/CSV/*.csv` with FrameTime, GPUTime, RenderThreadTime and GameThreadTime per frame; `csvprofile start` followed by quitting the game left a truncated file (227 rows). Runs taken back to back, before and after, were steadier than single runs. `stat unit` set the same way is drawn into the screenshot hook's frames. Do not count frames from the log's `[frame]` column: it wraps at 1000.
- **To learn what one part costs on a busy machine, switch it off and on inside one run.** Run on 5.9.0, 12 November 2026, with two other editors drawing on the same graphics chip. Separate runs of the same scene read 41 and 66 ms a frame; a step list in one `-game` run that set an off switch, waited a second and averaged frame time by the wall clock over 3 s, for each part in turn and twice round, put every row under the same load. Even so two rounds of one setting differed by up to 25 ms, and only one saving repeated (shadows of four spot lights with eight animated characters under them, 4.5 ms). Give every costly part of a look its own switch from the start.

## 5. Recording footage: exact frames, moving cameras

Verified on 5.9.0 on a Mac while recording two trailers (2,430 frames over 14 takes, none skipped). The recorder was one project script run through `uemcp.py py`; a shell script and a list of takes re-ran a whole shoot in one go.

- **The shipped video recorder does not run on a Mac.** `SceneCaptureToolset.SceneCaptureToolset.StartViewportRecording` (and `RecordViewport`) fail with "Video recording is not supported on Mac: it needs Windows Media Foundation." Their schema is otherwise what you would want (`source: GameViewport`, `maxResolution`, `frameRate`, an MP4 under `/Saved/Recordings`); not tested on Windows.
- **Exact 30 frames a second from stills.** Set `t.OverrideFPS 30`: every engine frame then advances the game by exactly 1/30 s whatever the real frame time is. In one `execute_python` script, wait for `unreal.SystemLibrary.get_frame_count()` to change, then `await call_tool("SceneCaptureToolset.SceneCaptureToolset.CaptureGameViewport", world=None, maxResolution=1920)`. The capture finished inside the frame it was asked in: the frame counter read the same before and after on every one of 2,430 captures. Log the frame number and `GameplayStatics.get_time_seconds` per capture; a skipped frame would show as a step of 2.
- **What `call_tool` returns inside a script:** a dict, not JSON text. For the capture it is `{"image": {"mimeType": "image/png", "data": <base64>}, "width", "height", "metadata", "warnings"}`; write `base64.b64decode(result["image"]["data"])` to disk from the script. `ListWorlds` comes back as `{"returnValue": [...]}`.
- **Speed and size.** About 4 captures a second at 1803 x 1608 (about 2 while a video render ran on the same machine). `t.MaxFPS 12` was set so the engine did not run ahead; it was not needed for correctness. The image is the viewport's shape: crop the centred 16:9 (1802 x 1014 here) and scale. Put `t.OverrideFPS` and `t.MaxFPS` back to 0 in a `finally`; if Play In Editor dies mid-take the script cannot, so reset them with `ConsoleToolset.SetCVarValue`.
- **Sounds for the footage.** Stepped footage has no usable live sound. The game's event log gives each hit and bounce a game time; with a fixed step, frame = (event time - first frame's time) x 30 + 1, so the game's own sound files can be laid on the right frame afterwards. Fifteen hits placed this way measured 40 to 60 ms after their picture frame, which is one frame of intended delay plus each file's attack.
- **A camera for a take.** `summon CameraActor` from the player controller spawns into the play world (the editor's spawn calls refuse during play); set `constrain_aspect_ratio` and `aspect_ratio` on its component, `set_view_target_with_blend(camera, 0.0)`, and give the view back to the game's camera afterwards. For a crane, orbit or tilt, call `set_actor_location_and_rotation` once per captured frame with the pose for that frame. Scout with one low-resolution still per candidate camera before recording: three of twelve first guesses had to be moved or dropped (two side cameras sat in the umpire's chair, one was too close to the scoreboard).
- **`showhud`** toggles the whole HUD for clean shots in Play In Editor; it is a toggle, so send it again afterwards. It reaches the HUD only from the editor or the MCP console tool: from a game's own step file (`GEngine->Exec`) it did nothing, and a packaged build has no MCP. Give the HUD its own hide console variable (`game.Hud.Hide`) for `-game` and packaged runs.
- **Another screen shape without a build.** To see the game at a phone's upright shape, set the game camera's `aspect_ratio` from Python during Play In Editor (`mode.gym_camera.get_component_by_class(unreal.CameraComponent).set_editor_property("aspect_ratio", 9 / 19.5)`, with `constrain_aspect_ratio` already on). The viewport pillarboxes to that shape with the game's real rendering and temporal history, which the offscreen `Capture` tool does not give; crop the centred `height x aspect` strip from `CaptureGameViewport`. `FieldOfView` stays horizontal, so a 34 degree camera at 9:19.5 sees about 67 degrees vertically: the court needs a higher, steeper camera, not the landscape one turned. A script that changes camera console variables on a paused game must let it tick before pausing again, or every capture is the previous camera (three identical stills here).
- **A 16:9 capture without cropping the game's framing: play in a floating window.** Run on 5.9.0, 12 November 2026. Set `new_window_width` and `new_window_height` to 1920 and 1080 on `unreal.get_default_object(unreal.LevelEditorPlaySettings)`, then `StartPIE` with `options={"playMode": "PlayMode_InEditorFloating"}`. `CaptureGameViewport` with `maxResolution` 1920 then returned 1920 x 1100, the HUD laid out for that shape; the centred 1920 x 1080 loses ten rows top and bottom. In the nearly square level viewport the same game needed a 16:9 crop that cut its HUD.
  - Rate: 3.9 captures a second at 1920 x 1100 with a second editor, a packaging job and Blender renders on the machine; 24 shots with no skipped engine frame.
  - The window takes keys. It opens on the owner's screen and can take keyboard focus; one stray key reached the editor during a take here (it changed nothing in the picture). Tell the owner not to click in it, and look at every clip before keeping it.
- **Scout a shot at one frame in N before recording it.** With `t.OverrideFPS 30` an uncaptured frame costs almost nothing, so capturing every 45th or 60th frame shows a whole 10 s shot in five to seven stills in about 10 s of wall time. Five of twelve shots scouted here were reframed (an awning and a string of lanterns in front of an orbit, characters cropped in an asset viewer).
- **Engine warnings are drawn into captures.** The orange "Multiple directional lights are competing..." line was in every capture of one level. `DisableAllScreenMessages` before the take removes it; `EnableAllScreenMessages` afterwards.
- **Move the game's own camera, not one of your own, when the look hangs things on it.** Run on 5.9.0, 12 November 2026. A look rig here placed its rain cards in front of the game's camera actor and wrote grain, bloom and exposure onto that camera's lens every tick, so a summoned camera showed neither. The game already read its camera's offsets from console variables each frame (`game.Cam.Back`, `Side`, `Up`, `Ahead`, `Across`, `Fov`); setting those once per captured frame gave side-on, face-on, low and crane tracking shots with the rain and the film look intact. Read the values first and put them back in the shot's `finally`.
- **Stopping the client does not stop the take.** Killing the shell that sent an `execute_python` script leaves the script running in the editor until it ends, with Play In Editor still up. Stop sending shots, wait until the frame folder stops growing, then stop the play session.
- **A closed play window ends the take with a stale world.** One take in 60 died with `Cannot nativize 'World' as 'WorldContextObject'` 5 s in: the floating play window had been closed by hand, and every later call used the dead world. A wrapper that goes on to the next shot loses one clip, not the run; re-record that shot.
- **Film with no editor and no window: the game by itself, drawn off screen.** Run on 5.9.0 on a Mac, 12 November 2026: 13 shots, 3,750 frames, every frame present, nothing on the owner's screen. It was also about three times faster than capturing over the editor's MCP (a 330-frame shot took about 90 s including start-up and encoding, against about 110 s for 300 frames).
  - The run: the editor binary with `/abs/MyGame.uproject /Game/Maps/<Level> -game -windowed -ResX=1920 -ResY=1080 -ForceRes -RenderOffScreen -nosplash -unattended -nosound -ModelContextProtocolPort=<port>`. Screenshots come out at exactly 1920 x 1080, so nothing is cropped.
  - Use it for every quick still too, not only footage, and start it under its own name (`ARGV0=MyShotGame <editor binary> ...` in zsh) so relaunch scripts that look for the editor binary neither count nor stop it. On 2026-11-13 the owner, working in other apps, reported that each windowed `-game` capture run took their mouse: the project's `bCaptureMouseOnLaunch=True` and `CapturePermanently_IncludingInitialMouseDown` grab the cursor the moment the new window opens. With `-RenderOffScreen` no window opens and `FScreenshotRequest` still writes a 1920 x 1080 frame.
  - **`-RenderOffScreen` hides the window; it does not keep the game off the mouse. The game needs a guard for it.** A short run left the owner's browser in front, but on 17 November 2026 a two-minute `-game -RenderOffScreen` check still took the owner's cursor: outside the editor the player controller set `FInputModeGameOnly`, and the project's input settings capture permanently and lock on capture. The guard, in the player controller's `BeginPlay`: when `GIsEditor` or `FParse::Param(FCommandLine::Get(), TEXT("RenderOffScreen"))`, use `FInputModeGameAndUI` with `EMouseLockMode::DoNotLock` and `SetHideCursorDuringCapture(false)`, and for the off-screen case also set the game viewport's `SetMouseCaptureMode(EMouseCaptureMode::NoCapture)` and `SetMouseLockMode(EMouseLockMode::DoNotLock)`. Checked off screen on a Mac with simulated fingers the same day; a packaged build started with the flag takes the same path, but was not run. For a check that needs no picture, `-nullrhi` opens no window at all. Say in chat before any standalone run.
  - **Tell the owner a capture has started, without taking focus.** The owner asked "is there a way to at least indicate that a capture is running". A macOS notification from `osascript -e 'display notification ...'` shows in the corner and leaves the front app alone; send at most one every few minutes across every session's runs, through one stamp file (`scripts/capture_notice.sh`).
  - **Give every unattended run its own time limit.** A run whose step list never arrived (an empty here-document) had no `quit` and ran until the shell's 10-minute limit; it was stopped by process name (from the record).
  - **The first frames carry the engine's loading text, and start-up time varies.** "Preparing Animation Sequences" and "Preparing Textures" were drawn in the corner of the first stills of two runs, and a published devlog step had to be shot again. Start-up varied by about a second, so a still timed in seconds after launch took three batches to land inside a 0.8 s beat. Time each still by game state (a step that waits `until <state>`), and look for the text before keeping a frame. Whether `DisableAllScreenMessages` hides it was not tried.
  - The frames: the game needs a hook of its own that takes one `FScreenshotRequest::RequestScreenshot(path, true, false)` per tick for N ticks; with `t.OverrideFPS 30` that is one picture per 1/30 s of game time. A hook that reads one line of a step file per tick cannot move the camera during such a run, so each shot holds one camera offset from the hero.
  - A `-game` run still starts the MCP server, but with no Python tool: only the console tools. `ConsoleToolset.RunConsoleCommand {"Command": "showhud"}` hid the HUD; the same word in the game's own step file did nothing, because `GEngine->Exec` does not reach the player's HUD.
  - One port per run. A second `-game` run started on the same MCP port straight after the first came up with no server, three times out of eight; bind-test ports upward from a base and pass the first free one. A `-game` run left on the default port takes the main editor's: two baseline runs bound 8000 before any editor did, and one held it 23 s after it quit. A `-run=pythonscript` commandlet starts no server.
  - Give the process its own `argv[0]` (`subprocess.Popen([name, ...], executable=<editor binary>)`), or scripts that find "the" editor with `pgrep` will count it.
- **Film from the packaged game, and get its sound back from its own log.** Run on 5.9.0 on a Mac, 14 November 2026, from a packaged Development build (no editor, no Python): the game's own step-file hook ran `t.OverrideFPS 30` and a `seq` of one `FScreenshotRequest` per tick, with `-RenderOffScreen -nosound`. 7,740 frames at 1920 x 1080 in 29 minutes (about 4.4 a second, 3.1 MB a PNG), none skipped, 0 errors, with the HUD on.
  - `getall <Class> <Property>` works in a packaged Development build, from the step file or `-ExecCmds`, and prints the value of every live instance to the log. The game's sound actor kept a string of every sound it played with its time (`"12.345 jump; ..."`) and that time as a property; a `getall` of both straight after each run of frames gave each frame's time (frame i of N is at the printed time - (N-1-i)/30). With `-nosound` the game still logged every sound it asked for.
  - Checked against the picture: an explosion's flash began on the frame its logged sound mapped to. Laying the game's own files at those frames, with its own volume table, gave a soundtrack in sync with no live recording; the loops (score, ambience) were laid by hand at the levels the game's mixer gives each state, since the log only lists one-shots.
  - The log string was capped at 24,000 characters by the game; four minutes of play came to 15,000, so one dump per run of frames was enough.
  - Open: two days later the same route, on a later packaged build, ran at 3 to 10 frames a second and never ran its steps, twice, and the cause was not found (from the record). Keep a second route (the editor binary with `-game`) ready.
- **Shoot each cut from one pinned commit.** One trailer pass spanned three commits as the game changed under it; each move meant stopping, moving the worktree, a clean build and a relaunch, and the 44 s cut mixes two builds. Pin the commit for the pass, or shoot it all again.
- **Level the footage's sound by measurement.** Measure the mix (`ffmpeg -af ebur128`), apply one gain to the target, and limit; automatic loudness normalisation let a quiet opening and a loud end drift apart. For a gameplay video, -14 LUFS integrated with the limiter at -3 dB before the AAC encode: with a ceiling of -1.5 dB the encoded file came out at +0.3 dB true peak. Say whether anyone has listened to the mix; a number is not a listen.
- **Rain and grain make footage big.** A 12 s clip of the dressed film look came to about 38 MB where a greybox clip was 3 MB at the same quality setting (from the record). Plan disk and upload sizes by the look, not only the length.
- **A take can still be lost.** One Play In Editor session ended 28 frames into a take with no `StopPIE` in the log. A click into the game window had happened 14 s earlier and another session's read-only poll 3 s earlier; the cause was not established. Tell every session to stay off the editor, reads included, and the owner not to click into it.
